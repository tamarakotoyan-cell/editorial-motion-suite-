# 04 — Graphics, imagery and data

What goes on the frame besides the words.

---

## Pick one graphic register per post and hold it

A post is illustrated, photographic, collaged, or data-led. Not two of those, and never
four.

| Register | Reads as | Held by |
|---|---|---|
| Illustration | Considered, editorial, safe on any subject | One illustrator, one palette, one line weight |
| Photography | Evidence | Full-bleed frames, no dimming |
| Collage | Argumentative, energetic | Cutouts on torn colour blocks, visible grain |
| Data | Authoritative | One chart at article-headline scale |

Mixing registers is what makes the rejected infographics read as clip-art assemblies: an
isometric 3D block beside a flat tick icon beside a numbered circle beside a dashed
connector, in one frame.

**One register per composition, and the ground is part of the register.** Paper structure,
creases and bleed-through are collage devices. They belong under cutouts on torn blocks;
they do not belong behind a photographic frame, where they turn evidence into a mood board.
A photographic frame's ground is the photograph.

### The one deliberate exception: a foreign register as the focal point

**One element may sit in a register foreign to the rest, when that difference *is* the
focal point.** This is `07-focal-point`'s register carrier, and it is the only sanctioned
way to mix.

> **vox-lazy-hand** *(not on disk — see `good example: annotations.md`)* — flat vector
> speech bubbles, a halftone dot field,
> and one duotone **photographic** hand holding the phone. The hand is where the eye lands,
> and it lands there because it is the only photograph in a vector frame.

The test is arithmetic: **exactly one element, and it is the thing the frame is about.**
Two photographs among the vectors is not a focal device, it is the clip-art assembly this
section opened by banning. If you cannot say which single element is the exception, there
isn't one.

## The three picture structures — name one before you start

Almost every strong reference frame is one of three things, and they are not blendable. The
failure mode is starting none of them and arriving at a moodboard.

**Structure A — one photograph carries the frame.** Full-bleed, undimmed, hard crop; type
set into the region the picture already made dark. The Atlantic covers, `nyt-fullbleed-
photo.png`. One asset, and the composition is a crop decision.

**Structure B — dense collage.** Many photographed elements at wildly different scales,
overlapping, occluding, four or more layers deep, on torn colour blocks. The NYT democracy
cover, the Vox annual report, `nyt-cutout-collage.png`.

⛔ **Precondition on B: roughly ten cut elements available, or do not attempt it.** Below
that the frame cannot reach the scale disparity and the occlusion the structure depends on,
and what arrives instead is four photographs arranged politely on a field — a pinboard.
Faking the density with vector shapes is precisely what reads as Canva. With fewer than ten
cut elements, build A — or C, if one of them cuts on its silhouette and the story has a
glyph vocabulary.

**Cut elements, not torn rectangles.** A rectangular photo crop behind a ragged mask is
still a rectangle: the tear is on the edge of the frame rather than on the edge of the
subject, and the eye reads a stack of torn prints instead of a collage. B needs subjects cut
on the silhouette. A torn edge is a device for the **colour blocks underneath** them and for
large masses, not a substitute for cutting out.

**Structure C — layered editorial.** One silhouette-cut, halftoned photographic subject
among flat single-colour glyph satellites, with the loud texture held inside a window shape
— or a second treated mass that seats the subject — on a calm paper ground. The Vox
"lazy but effective" hand, the CRT tile, the 19 Aug 2026 proof tile (albo-75-tile.html, pilot folder). **One**
cutout, three to six glyphs, one window or one mass. It is the foreign-register exception,
shape-as-a-window and the worked surface run together, and it is where the collage energy
comes from when the collage assets do not exist. Full grammar — layer order, texture zoning,
the satellite tests, the ground-mass tests — in `08-layered-editorial`.

⛔ **Precondition on C: the subject cuts on its silhouette, and there is a glyph vocabulary
for the story.** A subject that has to be cropped through is Structure A. A subject with
nothing to touch it floats clear and stickers on. A second photographic *subject* is B, and
B's precondition applies in full.

### Routing — decidable before design starts

The choice is made from what has been supplied, not from what the brief wants. Count the
assets, then read down:

| You have | Build | The wrong choice, and how it fails |
|---|---|---|
| One strong supplied photograph, and the story *is* evidence — the picture makes the point on its own | **A** | Forced into C: cropped through instead of cut, or a subject that was the whole scene now floating on paper. Forced into B: one photograph fragmented into a fake collage. Type-led instead: the evidence thrown away |
| One cuttable subject plus a glyph vocabulary the story owns | **C** | Forced into A: a cutout blown up to full-bleed with no scene behind it, then dimmed to 40–60% so type can sit on it — the wallpaper failure. Forced into B: the subject buried under invented density; nothing isolated, no focal point (`06-U`) |
| One cuttable subject and *no* glyph vocabulary | **Draw one** — three to six flat glyphs the story owns — and build **C**; if the story owns no objects, **type- or data-led with the cutout as the single foreign-register element** (the exception above), placed to break an edge | Scaled up to A: a cutout is not a full-frame photograph — blown up it is a sticker with nothing behind it. Built as C without satellites: the subject floats clear |
| Roughly ten silhouette cutouts in one register | **B** | Forced into A or C: nine assets unused, or eleven satellites where six was the ceiling — the sticker sheet |
| Two to seven photographic elements | **A or C, using one** — never B | Built as B anyway: the **pinboard** — four photographs arranged politely, scale disparity 1.6× not 5×, no occlusion, nothing breaking an edge (`06-V`). Density faked with vector shapes reads as Canva |
| None of the above | **Type-led or data-led** | Forced into any picture structure: a stock photograph standing in for evidence, or a cutout nobody cut. If the finding is one number it is a stat tile, not a chart |

State the structure in the five-line plan's *layout* line. If it cannot be named there, the
assets have not been counted.

## Illustration

- **One illustrator, one palette, one line weight across the whole post.** The palette is
  four to six colours and it is fixed at the cover.
- **Visible texture.** Stipple, grain, halftone, pencil. Flat vector illustration is the
  generated-design tell.
- **On the field, not in a box.** No container, no shadow, no card.
- **Break an edge.** The illustration runs off the frame in most compositions.
- **A hairline groundline is allowed** where objects need seating —
  `good example: nyt-illustration-over-headline.png` sits three buildings on one, and
  it is the only rule on the canvas.

Icons are not illustration. A set of small pictograms standing in for concepts — a
lightbulb for ideas, a rocket for growth — is decoration doing meaning's job, and it is
banned. The exception is a pictogram used as a **unit in a count** (see below), where the
icon *is* the data.

## Photography

**Full strength, or not at all.**

- Photo frames are **full-bleed and undimmed**, held as their own frame, with a hard crop.
  The photograph is evidence making its own point, not atmosphere behind the type.
- **Place the type where the photograph is already dark.** This is the first move, before
  any plate is considered. `good example: nyt-fullbleed-photo.png` puts white serif in
  the dark top third and adds nothing else.
- Only when no dark region exists: a **hard-edged type plate at a frame edge**, sized to
  the copy. Never a full-frame scrim.

⛔ **Never a photograph at 40–60% opacity behind the content as wallpaper.** It is too
present to be neutral ground and too suppressed to be evidence, so all it does is lower
contrast on everything in front of it. Alternate registers instead: a graphic frame, then a
photographic frame, each at full strength.

## Cutouts

The highest-risk element in static work and the one the rejected set gets wrong most often.

- **Cut on the silhouette.** Never a rectangular crop through a subject.
  `bad example: socceroos-cover-cutout.jpg` cuts square through a scarf mid-fabric.
- **Scale is the device.** Vary the size of cutouts across a frame. Uniform-scale cutouts
  read as a sticker sheet.
- **Overlap builds depth, not shadows.** Layer cutouts over each other and over colour
  blocks. `good example: nyt-cutout-collage.png` builds six layers of depth with no
  shadow anywhere.
- **Copy and cutouts do not overlap.** If they must share space, the cutout goes behind and
  the copy sits over a clear region of it.
- **Crop at the canvas edge deliberately or not at all.** A subject cut off mid-torso at the
  frame edge is either a decision that the composition supports, or an accident. It is
  usually an accident.
- **Torn and ragged edges** on the colour blocks beneath cutouts, not clean rectangles.

## Every raster asset goes through the same pipeline

Mandatory, in order, for every photograph in a composition. It is what makes seven images
shot by seven people in seven rooms belong to one sheet — and skipping it is the reason a
collage of good photographs still looks like a collage of other people's photographs.

1. **Key on the channel that separates subject from ground.** Whichever channel gives the
   cleanest separation, not luminance by default.
2. **Harden the alpha.** A soft matte is what makes a cutout look pasted. Threshold it.
3. **Desaturate or duotone** — one map, applied to every asset in the composition.
4. **Mask with the composition's shared tile**, at the same size and origin as the ground's.
5. **Torn foot, or a frame break.** Every asset does one or the other; none of them simply
   stops.

### Duotone, not greyscale

Map to **two brand values**, and **the highlight end must remain a colour**. A map running
shadow `0.07` to highlight `0.95 / 0.93 / 0.87` is greyscale with a rounding error: the
highlight is within a couple of points of white on all three channels, and the result reads
as a black-and-white photograph sitting on a warm ground rather than an image belonging to
it. Push the highlight to the actual bone value and let the shadow carry a hue.

The test: sample the lightest pixel of a treated asset. If it is neutral, the duotone did
not happen.

### Placement comes from the measured subject box

Position and crop from the **subject's** bounding box, never the file's frame. A portrait
centred in its own PNG and a portrait sitting in the left third of its PNG will land in
different places from identical CSS, and the second one reads as a mistake nobody can name.
Measure the subject, then place.

### Devices

One or two per composition, not the list:

- **One groundline for a set of cutouts.** Several subjects seated on a single hairline read
  as one scene; each floating at its own height reads as a sticker sheet.
- **Torn edges on large masses**, where a mass is big enough for the tear to have structure.
  A tear across 60px is a serration.
- **Type as a window** — the picture showing through the counters of the display type.
- **A shape as a window.** More general and usually stronger: one silhouette or geometric
  form, cut from the ground, holding whatever the frame is about. The Orwell head is a
  cream silhouette holding a dense collage on a flat halftone field — the collage would be
  noise anywhere else on that canvas, and inside the shape it is the subject. Works with
  emptiness too: the same silhouette holding nothing reads as absence.
- **An accent keyline on one cutout**, at hairline weight, on the one that carries the point.
- **A ghosted artefact behind** — a second, much larger copy of one element at low contrast,
  giving the composition a back plane. Where the artefact is set in type, it is a mark and
  not copy: give it `aria-hidden="true"`, so the linter holds it to the picture checks
  rather than the text ones and a screen reader skips the duplicate.
- **Two-corner anchoring.** Emptiness reads as composed when both far corners are held and
  as an unfinished frame when neither is. This is the cheapest fix for a composition that
  passes every rule and still floats: put weight in opposite corners and let the diagonal
  between them carry the space.

### Torn edges are masks, not paths

A tear is a **turbulence-displaced mask** — long wavelength across the tear, short down it,
one seed per fragment — or it is a photograph of a real torn edge. Never a polygon. See
`06-anti-patterns` **T**.

Two mechanics that bite: a CSS mask defaults to auto-size and repeat, which tiles the tear
into plaid, so set `mask-size:100% 100%; mask-repeat:no-repeat`. And `feTurbulence` writes
noise into **alpha** as well as RGB, so under `mask-mode:luminance` the mask is luminance ×
alpha and the whole fragment drops to about half opacity — pin alpha to 1.

## Charts

**Form first.** Pick the chart type from the data's job — magnitude, identity, polarity,
change over time, or a single headline. **If the finding is one number, it is a stat tile,
not a chart.**

- **The title is the finding, not the metric name.** "This is how Coles and Woolworths
  switch promotions in sync", not "Coca-Cola 1.25L price, Mar–Jun 2025".
- **Direct labelling, never a legend.** Put the series name on the mark, in a chip filled
  with the series colour. Guardian's chart labels "Coles" and "Woolworths" on the lines.
- **Hairline gridlines** one shade off the ground, or none. Never heavy, never dashed.
- **No chart container.** No border, no card, no background panel. The chart sits on the
  frame's ground.
- **Full width at article-headline scale.** A chart shrunk into a corner is a chart nobody
  will read at thumbnail size.
- **Label selectively** — the endpoint, the extreme, the one series that matters. A value
  printed on every point is a table.

### The mark must equal the number

Every mark in one chart shares a single scale: **length ÷ value is constant across the
series.** Compute geometry from the datum; never draw marks that look about right and type
the numbers on afterwards.

```html
<div class="bar" style="--v: 62"></div>
<style>.bar{ width: calc(var(--v) * 1% * var(--plot-scale)); }</style>
```

Corollaries: **no axis without a scale** — an axis is a promise about how to read the
marks. And **a value label is not a substitute for a correct mark** — if the number must be
read to know the magnitude, a sentence would have done the job better.

#### Percentage widths break the moment marks nest

The snippet above is correct for a flat series and silently wrong for a grouped one,
because a percentage resolves against the **parent**, not the plot. Put a 7 and a 21 inside
a group that is itself 28% of the row and each sub-segment sizes against the group — the
pair divides correctly *relative to each other* and lands at roughly a third of its true
length against everything else.

It looks plausible, which is why it survives review. It has now been caught twice: once in
a hand-built artifact and once in a Canva rebuild of the same chart, where the split at the
foot of the bar came out visibly short and nothing in the file said why.

Use flex-grow instead. Growth distributes against the row, gaps are subtracted before
distribution, and nesting is correct by construction:

```html
<div class="row">                                  <!-- 28 + 36 + 36 = 100 -->
  <div class="grp" style="--v:28">
    <i class="seg" style="--v:7"></i><i class="seg" style="--v:21"></i>
  </div>
  ...
</div>
```
```css
.row  { display:flex; gap:4px }
.grp  { display:flex; gap:3px; flex:var(--v) 1 0; min-width:0 }
.seg  { flex:var(--v) 1 0; min-width:0 }
```

`min-width:0` matters: without it a flex item refuses to shrink below its content width, so
a long label inside a narrow segment silently widens the mark and breaks the scale.

⛔ **Never padding on a flex-grow mark.** With `flex-basis:0` the browser distributes the
free space by grow factor and then adds each item's padding back, so every mark gains the
same constant and length ÷ value stops being one number. It fails hardest where it matters
most: measured on a real chart, a 9% category with a 14px inset each side drew 32% too long,
while the 63% category beside it was off by 4% and looked fine. The inset goes on a child.

```css
.seg   { flex:var(--v) 1 0; min-width:0 }   /* geometry */
.seg > span { padding:0 14px }              /* inset */
```

**The check, and run it on the rendered file rather than the source:** divide each mark's
pixel width by its value. It is the same number for all of them or the chart is decoration
with numbers on it.

### Direct labelling, when the mark is too narrow to hold its label

"Never a legend" is the rule; this is the mechanism, because the hard case is not a line
chart with room beside it. It is a segmented bar where one segment is 7% of the plot —
about 60px — and its label is the word "strongly", which is not.

Three placements, in order of preference:

1. **Inside the mark.** The block carries its own name and figure. Best where the mark is
   large: it removes a whole row from the layout and nothing can drift out of register.
2. **A hairline leader from the mark to a label below it.** For sub-segments too narrow to
   hold type. The leader drops from the segment's centre, doglegs where the label had to
   shift to stay inside the frame, and lands on the label's centre. This is the device the
   reference set uses everywhere — Nightingale's dotted connectors, Accurat's city leaders,
   the leader lines on a labelled donut.
3. **A chip on the mark**, filled with the series colour. For line series.

⛔ **A stacked list of value–label pairs under a chart is a legend wearing a disguise.**
`7 strongly / 21 somewhat / 27 neither` set as a block beneath the marks is the same
failure as a swatch key: the reader has to map left-to-right marks onto top-to-bottom text
and hold the mapping in their head. It reads as tidy, which is why it survives. Anchor each
label to its own mark, or put it inside.

**Position every annotation from the measured mark, never from a hardcoded offset.** A
leader nudged into place with `left: 32%` points at nothing the moment the data, the
container width or the font changes — and a leader pointing at the wrong segment is worse
than no leader.

```js
const base = plot.getBoundingClientRect();
const s    = seg.getBoundingClientRect();
const cx   = s.left - base.left + s.width / 2;        // centre of the mark
const left = Math.min(Math.max(cx - lw / 2, 0), base.width - lw);  // clamp to frame
```

Re-run on resize, and once more after `document.fonts.ready` so webfont metrics have
settled.

### Annotation devices

Distinct from the emphasis devices in `03-colour-and-ground` — those mark a *word*, these
mark a *quantity*. One per frame, from:

- **The hand-drawn circle**, for *look here*. An irregular path in the accent with a short
  callout. Keep the wobble; a clean `<ellipse>` defeats it.
- **A span rule**, for *these are equal* or *these belong together*. A hairline across the
  marks it covers, with a tick at each end and one where the two halves meet — so a reader
  sees the halves are identical rather than being told. This is the right device for a tie
  and the circle is not.
- **A callout chip.** A hard-edged accent block with knockout type, anchored to the left
  edge of the span it explains. Set it at **24px or above** so white on a mid-chroma accent
  clears the WCAG large-text threshold; below that the same colour pair fails.

### Count rather than measure

Under roughly 200, draw one mark per unit. A hundred dots in a 10×10 grid reads as *a
hundred things*; a bar of height 100 reads as an abstraction.

- Default to **one dot = one per cent**, so a share is literally countable against a
  denominator that is on screen.
- **Never show a share with no denominator.** If 63 dots are shown and the other 37 are
  gone, the figure has nothing to be a share *of*.
- **Emphasise in place rather than extracting.** Brighten the group and drop the rest to
  ~40%, so the reader also sees *where* in the hundred they sit.

### Stat tiles

One number, at display size, in the sans, with a short label beneath it and the source
line at the foot. That is the entire component.

Banned: a one-bar bar chart, a two-slice pie, a three-across row of equally-weighted
figures where one of them was the point.

## Source lines

**Non-negotiable for research work.** Source and sample size, small, muted, bottom-aligned,
above the 1.25%-of-canvas-height floor from `02-typography`.

State the base whenever it changes between slides. A new question or a new sample is a fact
the reader needs on the frame, not a footnote.

## Self-check

- One graphic register, held across the post — and does the ground belong to it?
- Structure A, B or C, named before starting from the asset count? If B, are there ten cut
  elements? If C, exactly one photographic subject, and does any mass pass `08`'s tests?
- Two to seven photographic elements on the frame? That is the pinboard — build A or C with
  one of them.
- Did every raster asset go through the same pipeline, with the same duotone map?
- Is the lightest pixel of a treated asset still a colour, or did it come out greyscale?
- Are the tears masks rather than paths, and are the cuts on silhouettes rather than frames?
- Are both far corners held?
- Does the illustration carry texture, and does it break an edge?
- Are there icons standing in for concepts?
- Is any photograph dimmed behind content?
- Is every cutout cut on its silhouette, clear of the copy?
- Is the chart title the finding?
- Are series labelled directly, on the marks — or is there a value list doing a legend's job?
- Does length ÷ value hold constant across every mark, measured on the **rendered** file?
- Are any nested marks sized with percentages rather than flex-grow?
- Is every annotation placed from a measured mark rather than a fixed offset?
- Is there a denominator on screen for every share?
- Is the source and sample size present and legible?
